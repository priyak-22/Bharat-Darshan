Battle of Buxar (1764 CE)

Period: 1764 CE | Region: Buxar (on the banks of River Ganga, Bihar)

Background

The Battle of Buxar was fought on 22 October 1764 between the British East India Company and a combined army of Mir Qasim (Nawab of Bengal), Shuja‑ud‑Daula (Nawab of Awadh), and Mughal Emperor Shah Alam II. It followed the earlier Battle of Plassey (1757), after which the British had installed Mir Jafar as a puppet Nawab and tightened their grip over Bengal’s revenues. Mir Qasim, who succeeded Mir Jafar, tried to free Bengal from British interference by reforming the administration, improving the army, and challenging Company privileges, which led to open conflict.

After being defeated in several engagements and forced to flee, Mir Qasim formed an anti‑British alliance with Shuja‑ud‑Daula of Awadh and Shah Alam II, who had been wandering in exile after losing Delhi. This coalition aimed to roll back British influence and restore a degree of independence to Bengal and Awadh, but it ultimately faced a disciplined and better‑organized British force at Buxar.

Causes

  • Mir Qasim’s Reforms and Resistance: After becoming Nawab of Bengal, Mir Qasim tried to reduce Company interference by abolishing internal customs duties, reforming the army, and shifting his capital to Munger. These measures threatened British trade and revenue interests, leading to a breakdown in relations and open warfare.
  • British Expansion and Trade Privileges: The East India Company’s unchecked privileges, including tax‑free trade and control over key forts, disrupted local economies and undermined the authority of Indian rulers. The Company’s growing military presence and interference in succession disputes further angered regional powers.
  • Anti‑British Alliance: Mir Qasim, Shuja‑ud‑Daula, and Shah Alam II formed a coalition to challenge British dominance, hoping to use the Emperor’s symbolic legitimacy and the combined strength of Bengal and Awadh to restore balance. However, coordination between the allies was weak, and their strategy lacked unity.

Main Events

The British army, led by Major Hector Munro, numbered about 7,000–8,000 men, including European troops and sepoys, and was well‑trained and disciplined. The combined Indian forces of Mir Qasim, Shuja‑ud‑Daula, and Shah Alam II were much larger in number but suffered from poor coordination, outdated tactics, and internal rivalries. The battle took place on the banks of the Ganga near Buxar, a strategically important town controlling access to Bengal and the upper Gangetic plain.

On 22 October 1764, the British launched a well‑coordinated attack, using superior artillery and disciplined infantry to break the allied lines. The Indian forces were unable to mount an effective counter‑attack, and their formations began to disintegrate under sustained British fire. Mir Qasim fled the battlefield, Shuja‑ud‑Daula retreated toward Awadh, and Shah Alam II withdrew to Allahabad, leaving the British in control of the field.

Notable Figures

  • Major Hector Munro: British commander who led the East India Company’s troops to a decisive victory at Buxar. His disciplined use of artillery and infantry tactics demonstrated the growing military superiority of the Company over Indian armies.
  • Mir Qasim: Nawab of Bengal who attempted to resist British control through administrative reforms and military modernization. After his defeat, he lost his position and spent the rest of his life in exile, symbolizing the failure of independent resistance in Bengal.
  • Shuja‑ud‑Daula: Nawab of Awadh and a key ally in the anti‑British coalition, he brought substantial forces to the battle but was unable to coordinate effectively with his allies. After Buxar, he was forced to accept British terms and became a dependent ally.
  • Shah Alam II: Mughal Emperor whose defeat at Buxar further weakened imperial authority and confirmed the decline of the Mughal Empire. He later signed the Treaty of Allahabad (1765), granting the Diwani of Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa to the British.

Outcome

The British victory at Buxar confirmed their political dominance in eastern India and shattered the last major coalition capable of challenging Company power. In the aftermath, Shah Alam II was forced to sign the Treaty of Allahabad in 1765, granting the Diwani (right to collect revenue) of Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa to the British East India Company. This gave the Company direct control over the richest provinces of the Mughal Empire, providing a massive financial base for further expansion.

Mir Qasim was replaced as Nawab, Shuja‑ud‑Daula accepted British suzerainty over Awadh, and Shah Alam II remained a symbolic figurehead under British protection. The battle thus marked a decisive shift from indirect Company influence to direct territorial and fiscal control in large parts of northern India.

Impact and Significance

  • The Battle of Buxar marked the beginning of British political rule in India, as the Company became the de facto ruler of Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa through the Diwani grant.
  • It made the East India Company a major territorial power, transforming it from a trading corporation into a state‑like entity with revenue‑collecting and administrative functions.
  • The defeat of Shah Alam II and the anti‑British alliance reduced the Mughal Emperor to a symbolic ruler, accelerating the decline of the Mughal Empire and paving the way for British dominance.
  • Buxar strengthened British control over revenue and administration in eastern India, enabling further military campaigns and political interventions that would extend Company rule across much of the subcontinent.
