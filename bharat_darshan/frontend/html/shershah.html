Sher Shah Suri (1540–1545 CE)

Region: Northern India — Delhi, Bihar, Bengal, Punjab, and parts of Central India

Capital

Sher Shah Suri ruled from Delhi after defeating the Mughal emperor Humayun. He also developed Sasaram (in present-day Bihar) as an important center. Delhi remained the main administrative capital of his empire.

Background

Sher Shah Suri, originally named Farid Khan, was born in Sasaram, Bihar. He belonged to an Afghan family and rose through military service. He gained the title "Sher Khan" after reportedly killing a tiger. After defeating Humayun at the Battles of Chausa (1539) and Kannauj (1540), he established the Sur Empire in North India.

Founding and Rise

Sher Shah first served under Afghan rulers in Bihar and Bengal. Through strategic planning and strong leadership, he expanded his influence across eastern India. His victory over the Mughals allowed him to take control of Delhi and establish an efficient and centralized administration.

Religion

Sher Shah Suri followed Islam, but he adopted a practical and tolerant approach toward governance. He employed capable officials regardless of religion and focused more on administrative efficiency than on religious policy.

Economy and Administration

Sher Shah is best remembered for his administrative reforms. He introduced a well-organized land revenue system based on measurement of land and fair tax rates. He standardized the currency by introducing the silver “Rupiya,” which later influenced the modern Indian rupee. He also improved road networks, most notably the Grand Trunk Road, connecting Bengal to Punjab, facilitating trade and communication.

Notable Achievements

  • Land Revenue Reform – Introduced systematic land measurement and taxation.
  • Currency Reform – Issued the standardized silver Rupiya.
  • Grand Trunk Road – Improved and extended major trade routes.
  • Efficient Administration – Organized provinces under capable governors.

Political History

Sher Shah Suri’s reign was short but impactful. He strengthened military organization and improved governance. His reforms later influenced Mughal Emperor Akbar’s administration. Despite ruling for only five years, he laid the foundation for a strong centralized state.

Cultural Heritage

Tangible Heritage

The Tomb of Sher Shah Suri at Sasaram is a remarkable example of Indo-Islamic architecture. Built in the middle of a large water tank, it reflects a blend of Afghan and Indian architectural styles.

Intangible Heritage

Sher Shah’s legacy lies mainly in his administrative innovations. His revenue system, road construction, and postal services set standards for later rulers. His governance model influenced the Mughal Empire and shaped early modern Indian administration.

Death and End of Rule

Sher Shah Suri died in 1545 during the siege of Kalinjar Fort due to an accidental explosion of gunpowder. After his death, the Sur Empire weakened under his successors. In 1555, Humayun regained Delhi, leading to the restoration of Mughal rule.

Historical Sources and Evidence

Information about Sher Shah Suri comes from Persian chronicles, administrative records, coins, and architectural remains. His tomb at Sasaram and sections of the Grand Trunk Road provide important historical evidence of his reign.
