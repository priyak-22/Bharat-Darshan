Mewar Dynasty (c. 8th–18th century CE)

Region: Southern Rajasthan — Chittorgarh, Udaipur, Kumbhalgarh and surrounding Aravalli region

Capital

The early capital of Mewar was Chittorgarh, one of the largest hill forts in India. After repeated sieges, Maharana Udai Singh II founded Udaipur in 1559, which later became the permanent capital. Both cities were strategically protected by the Aravalli hills.

Background

Mewar was ruled by the Sisodia clan of Rajputs and is considered one of the most prestigious Rajput kingdoms. The dynasty traced its origin to the Guhila lineage, traditionally associated with Bappa Rawal. Mewar became a symbol of resistance and Rajput honor in medieval India.

Founding and Rise

The foundation of Mewar is attributed to Bappa Rawal in the 8th century. The kingdom rose to prominence under Rana Kumbha in the 15th century, who expanded its territory and strengthened fortifications. Later, Rana Sanga made Mewar a dominant Rajput power in North India.

Religion

The rulers of Mewar were devoted followers of Hinduism. They worshipped Eklingji (a form of Lord Shiva), who was considered the supreme ruler of the kingdom. Jainism also flourished under their patronage, and many temples were constructed.

Economy and Administration

The economy of Mewar was primarily agrarian. The rulers constructed lakes and reservoirs such as Lake Pichola to improve irrigation. Trade and handicrafts also contributed to revenue. The administration followed a feudal structure with local chiefs governing territories under the Maharana.

Notable Rulers

  • Bappa Rawal – Founder of the Sisodia rule in Mewar.
  • Rana Kumbha – Builder of Kumbhalgarh Fort and Vijay Stambh.
  • Rana Sanga – United Rajput clans and fought Babur at Khanwa.
  • Maharana Pratap – Famous for resisting Akbar and the Battle of Haldighati.

Political History and Achievements

Mewar faced invasions from Alauddin Khalji and later from the Mughals. Despite repeated attacks, it preserved its identity and autonomy. The construction of massive hill forts and preservation of Rajput traditions remain its major achievements.

Cultural Heritage

Tangible Heritage

Chittorgarh Fort

A UNESCO-listed hill fort symbolizing Rajput bravery. It witnessed three major sieges and acts of Jauhar.

Kumbhalgarh Fort

Built by Rana Kumbha. It has one of the longest walls in the world. The fort provided refuge during invasions.

City Palace, Udaipur

Overlooking Lake Pichola, this palace complex reflects Rajput architectural brilliance combined with Mughal influences.

Vijay Stambh

Built by Rana Kumbha to commemorate victory over the Sultan of Malwa. It is richly carved with Hindu deities and inscriptions.

Intangible Heritage

Mewar is known for its Rajput code of honor, martial traditions, ballads celebrating Maharana Pratap, and the Mewar school of miniature painting. Folk traditions, festivals, and rituals continue to preserve its legacy.

Decline and Fall

Continuous warfare weakened Mewar economically. Eventually, it entered into a treaty with the Mughal Empire. During British rule, it became a princely state. After Indian independence in 1947, Mewar merged into the Indian Union and became part of Rajasthan.

Historical Sources and Evidence

Information about Mewar comes from inscriptions, temple records, the Eklinga Mahatmya chronicle, Mughal texts such as the Akbarnama, and archaeological remains of forts and palaces.
