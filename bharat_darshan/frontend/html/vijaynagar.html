Vijayanagar Empire (1336–1646 CE)

Region: Southern India — Present-day Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu, and Kerala

Capital

The capital of the Vijayanagar Empire was Vijayanagara, located near present-day Hampi in Karnataka. The city was one of the largest and wealthiest cities in the world during the 15th and 16th centuries. It was strategically protected by hills and the Tungabhadra River.

Background

The Vijayanagar Empire was founded in 1336 CE by Harihara I and Bukka Raya I of the Sangama dynasty. It emerged after the decline of the Delhi Sultanate’s influence in the Deccan. The empire aimed to protect southern India from northern invasions and to preserve regional culture and traditions.

Founding and Rise

The empire expanded rapidly under the Sangama rulers. Later dynasties such as the Saluva, Tuluva, and Aravidu continued its rule. The empire reached its peak under Krishnadevaraya (1509–1529 CE), who expanded territory, strengthened administration, and encouraged literature and art. During his reign, Vijayanagar became the dominant power in South India.

Religion

The rulers of Vijayanagar were devoted to Hinduism, especially Vaishnavism and Shaivism. They were patrons of temples and religious institutions. However, they also maintained tolerance toward other religions, including Islam and Jainism.

Economy and Administration

The Vijayanagar economy was strong and diversified. Agriculture was supported by irrigation tanks and canals. Trade flourished with foreign merchants from Persia, Arabia, and Europe. The empire exported spices, textiles, precious stones, and horses. Administration was organized into provinces governed by Nayakas, who managed local revenue and military responsibilities.

Notable Rulers

  • Harihara I – Co-founder of the Vijayanagar Empire.
  • Bukka Raya I – Expanded the empire’s territory.
  • Krishnadevaraya – Greatest ruler who strengthened administration and promoted arts and literature.
  • Achyuta Deva Raya – Successor of Krishnadevaraya who maintained stability for some time.

Political History and Achievements

The empire successfully resisted the Bahmani Sultanate and other Deccan powers for many years. It became a center of cultural and economic prosperity. However, in 1565 CE, the combined forces of the Deccan Sultanates defeated Vijayanagar in the Battle of Talikota. Although the empire continued in reduced form, its political dominance declined after this defeat.

Cultural Heritage

Tangible Heritage

The empire left behind magnificent temple architecture. The famous Virupaksha Temple at Hampi is a remarkable example of Vijayanagar architecture. Other structures include the Vittala Temple, stone chariot, royal enclosures, and market streets. The ruins of Hampi are now a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Intangible Heritage

Vijayanagar promoted classical languages such as Telugu, Kannada, Sanskrit, and Tamil. Krishnadevaraya himself was a scholar and poet. Literature, music, dance, and temple rituals flourished. The empire played a key role in preserving South Indian culture and traditions.

Decline and Fall

The decline began after the Battle of Talikota in 1565 CE. The capital city was destroyed and plundered. Although the Aravidu dynasty continued to rule from Penukonda and later Chandragiri, the empire gradually weakened. By 1646 CE, Vijayanagar rule had effectively ended.

Historical Sources and Evidence

Information about the Vijayanagar Empire comes from inscriptions, temple records, foreign travelers such as Domingo Paes and Niccolò de Conti, and archaeological remains at Hampi. These sources provide detailed insights into administration, economy, and cultural life.
