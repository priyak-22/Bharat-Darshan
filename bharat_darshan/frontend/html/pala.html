Pala Dynasty (8th–12th Century CE)

Capital

The Pala rulers had multiple capitals including Gauda, Magadha, and Monghyr. Their control mainly covered Bengal and Bihar.

View Nalanda University

Background

The Pala Dynasty was founded in the mid-8th century in Bengal. It emerged after a period of political instability. The first ruler, Gopala, was elected by local chieftains to restore order.

The dynasty became one of the most powerful empires in eastern India and played a key role in spreading Buddhism across Asia.

Founding and Rise

Gopala founded the dynasty around 750 CE. His successor Dharmapala greatly expanded the empire. The Palas controlled Bengal, Bihar, and parts of Uttar Pradesh and Assam.

Under Dharmapala and Devapala, the empire reached its peak and became a dominant force in northern India.

Religion

The Pala rulers were strong supporters of Mahayana and Vajrayana Buddhism. They also showed tolerance toward Hinduism. Buddhist monasteries and universities flourished under their patronage.

Economy and Administration

Agriculture was the main source of revenue. Fertile lands of Bengal supported rice cultivation. Trade through river routes and ports contributed to economic growth.

The administration was monarchical. Provinces were governed by appointed officials. Land grants were given to Brahmins and Buddhist monasteries.

Notable Rulers

Gopala (c. 750 CE)

Founder of the dynasty. Elected by regional chiefs.

Dharmapala (c. 770–810 CE)

Expanded the empire and established control over Kannauj. Founded Vikramashila University.

Devapala (c. 810–850 CE)

Extended the empire further and strengthened international relations.

Political History

The Palas were involved in the Tripartite Struggle with the Gurjara-Pratiharas and Rashtrakutas for control of Kannauj.

Though initially successful, later rulers faced invasions and internal decline.

Achievements

  • Revival and promotion of Buddhism
  • Establishment of major universities
  • Development of Pala art and sculpture
  • International cultural exchanges with Southeast Asia

Intangible Heritage

The Pala dynasty significantly contributed to the spread of Buddhism to Tibet and Southeast Asia. Their support strengthened Mahayana and Vajrayana traditions. The intellectual traditions of Nalanda and Vikramashila shaped Buddhist philosophy for centuries.

Decline and Fall

The decline began in the 11th century due to weak successors, regional revolts, and invasions by the Sena dynasty. By the 12th century, Pala power ended in Bengal.
