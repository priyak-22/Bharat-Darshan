Civil Disobedience Movement (1930–1934 CE)

Period: 1930–1934 CE | Region: All India

Background

After the failure of earlier constitutional reforms and the boycott of the Simon Commission (1927–1928), Mahatma Gandhi launched the Civil Disobedience Movement to protest against British laws and assert the right to self‑rule. The Lahore Session of the Indian National Congress in December 1929 had already adopted the resolution of Poorna Swaraj (Complete Independence) and authorised a mass movement, which Gandhi was empowered to initiate at his discretion. In January 1930 Gandhi sent an ultimatum to Viceroy Lord Irwin with eleven modest demands (including reduction of land revenue, abolition of the salt tax, and release of political prisoners), warning that if they were ignored he would launch civil disobedience.

When the British government rejected these demands, Gandhi chose to break the Salt Law as the symbolic starting point of the movement, since salt was a basic necessity used by rich and poor alike and its taxation represented the economic exploitation of the masses.

Launch and Leadership

  • Launched by Mahatma Gandhi on March 12, 1930 with the historic Dandi March (Salt March), which formally began the Civil Disobedience Movement.
  • Gandhi, along with 78 carefully chosen satyagrahis, marched from Sabarmati Ashram in Ahmedabad to the coastal village of Dandi in Gujarat, covering about 390 km over 24 days.
  • On April 6, 1930, Gandhi picked up a handful of salt from the seashore, openly violating the British salt monopoly and inviting Indians across the country to follow his example.

The movement quickly spread across India, involving millions of people from all sections of society and transforming the freedom struggle into a truly mass‑based campaign. Gandhi remained the central leader, supported by Jawaharlal Nehru, Vallabhbhai Patel, Sarojini Naidu, and many other Congress figures who organised local satyagrahas and led protests.

Main Features

  • Refusal to pay taxes and obey unjust laws, especially the salt tax, but also land revenue and other colonial regulations in some regions.
  • Boycott of British goods, courts, educational institutions, and government services, with people withdrawing from official life and joining alternative, nationalist‑run structures.
  • Resignation from government services, municipal bodies, and other official posts, as well as the surrender of British titles and honours.
  • Peaceful protests, picketing of liquor shops and foreign‑goods shops, processions, and mass meetings, all conducted under Gandhi’s strict principle of non‑violence.

The movement also included the widespread promotion of Khadi and village industries, as Gandhi linked economic self‑reliance with political resistance.

Key Events

  • Dandi March (1930): The 24‑day march from Sabarmati to Dandi became a powerful symbol of defiance against British salt laws and inspired similar “salt satyagrahas” across India, including marches in Tamil Nadu (Trichinopoly–Vedaranyam), Kerala, Bengal, Maharashtra, and Punjab.
  • Gandhi–Irwin Pact (1931): After mass arrests and repression, Viceroy Lord Irwin agreed to talks with Gandhi, leading to the Gandhi–Irwin Pact in March 1931. Gandhi agreed to suspend the movement and attend the Second Round Table Conference in London, while the government released political prisoners and relaxed some restrictions.
  • Resumption of the Movement (1932–1934): When the Round Table talks failed and the British reimposed repressive measures, Gandhi revived the Civil Disobedience Movement in 1932. It continued in a more fragmented and localised form until April 1934, when he formally withdrew it to focus on constructive work and reorganisation.

Notable episodes included the brutal police lathi‑charge on satyagrahis at the Dharasana Salt Works led by Sarojini Naidu and the widespread participation of women, students, peasants, and tribals in protests and boycotts.

Impact and Significance

  • The Civil Disobedience Movement brought the demand for complete independence (Poorna Swaraj) to the forefront of Indian politics, moving beyond earlier calls for dominion status.
  • It mobilised women, students, peasants, workers, and even some zamindars on a large scale, giving the freedom struggle a broad social base and deepening political awareness across the country.
  • The movement strengthened Gandhi’s position as the undisputed national leader and demonstrated the power of non‑violent mass resistance as a tool against colonial rule.
  • Although it did not secure immediate independence, it weakened British authority, forced negotiations, and inspired later global anti‑colonial struggles by showing that a colonial empire could be challenged through disciplined, peaceful defiance.
