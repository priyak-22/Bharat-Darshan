Advent of the British (1615 CE)

Period: 1615 CE | Region: Surat, Madras, Bombay, Calcutta

Background

The British East India Company was founded in 1600 CE under a royal charter from Queen Elizabeth I to trade with the East Indies, and it first reached India in 1608 when its ships docked at Surat. In 1615 CE, Sir Thomas Roe arrived at the court of Mughal Emperor Jahangir as ambassador of King James I and secured formal permission for the Company to establish factories and trade freely in Mughal territories. This marked the beginning of a sustained British commercial presence in India, which gradually evolved from a trading enterprise into a political and military power.

Over the 17th century, the Company expanded its network of factories along both the western and eastern coasts, often negotiating with Mughal officials and regional rulers for land, customs privileges, and fortification rights. By the early 18th century, the British had become one of the dominant European powers in India, setting the stage for colonial rule.

Major Trading Posts

  • Surat: The Company’s first major factory in India was established at Surat in 1615 CE, after Thomas Roe obtained permission from Jahangir. Surat served as the initial headquarters on the western coast and a key gateway to the rich textile‑producing regions of Gujarat and northern India.
  • Madras (Chennai): In 1639 CE, the Company obtained a lease of the fishing village of Madraspatnam from local Nayak rulers and built Fort St. George, which later became the South Indian headquarters of the British. Madras emerged as a major centre for cotton‑textile trade and administration.
  • Bombay (Mumbai): Bombay came into English hands in 1661 CE as part of the dowry of Catherine of Braganza, and the Crown transferred it to the East India Company in 1665. The Company fortified the island and developed it into the principal western port, eventually replacing Surat as the main hub for trade on the west coast.
  • Calcutta (Kolkata): Calcutta was founded in 1690 CE by Job Charnock, who consolidated several villages into a fortified settlement on the Hooghly River. It grew into the capital of British India in the 18th century and the nerve centre of British political, military, and economic power in Bengal and beyond.

Administration

The British East India Company initially functioned as a private trading corporation governed by a Court of Directors in London, with subordinate councils and governors in India managing local affairs. Early administration focused on running factories, collecting customs, and negotiating with Indian rulers, but over time the Company developed its own army, judiciary, and revenue‑collection machinery.

By the mid‑18th century, after victories in battles such as Plassey (1757) and Buxar (1764), the Company began to rule large territories directly, collecting land revenue and administering justice in Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa. This hybrid structure—corporate body with sovereign powers—laid the foundation for British colonial rule in India, which was later formalized under the British Crown after the Government of India Act 1858.

Trade and Economy

  • The British traded Indian cotton and silk textiles, indigo, spices, saltpetre, and later opium, exporting them to Europe and re‑exporting them to Southeast Asia and China.
  • They imported British manufactured goods, metals, and luxury items, gradually shifting the balance of trade in favour of Britain and integrating India into a global imperial‑commercial system.
  • The Company established fortified settlements such as Fort St. George (Madras), Fort William (Calcutta), and Bombay Castle to protect trade, store goods, and house troops.
  • Ports like Bombay, Madras, and Calcutta became major nodes in a shipping network that linked India with Britain, the Persian Gulf, Southeast Asia, and China, transforming India into a key supplier of raw materials and a market for British manufactures.

Impact and Significance

  • The arrival of the British East India Company marked the beginning of long‑term British involvement in Indian politics, economy, and society, eventually leading to nearly two centuries of colonial rule.
  • Through alliances, wars, and treaties, the Company expanded its control from a few coastal enclaves to vast inland territories, reshaping the political map of India.
  • British rule introduced new administrative systems, legal codes, railways, and telegraph networks, which modernized parts of the economy but also deepened economic dependence on Britain.
  • India was transformed into a core component of the global British Empire, supplying raw materials, soldiers, and markets, while British institutions, education, and infrastructure left a lasting imprint on Indian society.
