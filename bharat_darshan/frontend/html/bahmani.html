Bahmani Sultanate (1347–1527 CE)

Region: Deccan Plateau — Present-day Karnataka, Maharashtra, Telangana, and Andhra Pradesh

Capital

The first capital of the Bahmani Sultanate was Gulbarga (renamed Ahsanabad by the rulers). Later, the capital was shifted to Bidar in the 15th century. Both cities became important political and cultural centers of the Deccan.

Background

The Bahmani Sultanate was the first independent Islamic kingdom in the Deccan region. It was founded in 1347 by Ala-ud-Din Bahman Shah after revolting against the Delhi Sultanate under Muhammad bin Tughlaq. The formation of the Bahmani state marked the beginning of a strong Deccan political identity separate from northern India.

Founding and Rise

Ala-ud-Din Bahman Shah established control over the Deccan with support from local nobles and military leaders. The empire expanded rapidly, engaging in frequent conflicts with the Vijayanagara Empire over control of the Raichur Doab. The Bahmani rulers strengthened administration, military organization, and trade networks across the Deccan.

Religion

The Bahmani rulers followed Islam and promoted Persian culture. Many scholars, poets, and administrators from Persia and Central Asia were invited to the Deccan. At the same time, the Sultanate governed a diverse population, including large Hindu communities who played important roles in administration and trade.

Economy and Administration

Agriculture formed the backbone of the economy, supported by land revenue systems and irrigation works. The kingdom was divided into provinces called “tarafs,” each governed by a noble. Trade flourished due to the Deccan’s strategic position connecting northern India with southern ports. Textile production, horse trade, and metalwork were significant economic activities.

Notable Rulers

  • Ala-ud-Din Bahman Shah – Founder of the Bahmani Sultanate.
  • Firuz Shah Bahmani – Known for administrative reforms and patronage of scholars.
  • Mahmud Gawan – Influential prime minister who reorganized the administration and expanded the empire.

Political History and Achievements

The Bahmani Sultanate played a central role in Deccan politics for nearly two centuries. Its rulers frequently clashed with the Vijayanagara Empire over territory and resources. The administration was reorganized under Mahmud Gawan, who improved revenue systems and military efficiency. The Sultanate became a major center of Persianate culture in India.

Cultural Heritage

Tangible Heritage

The architectural legacy of the Bahmani period includes the Gol Gumbaz and the Bidar Fort. Their architecture combined Persian, Turkish, and local Deccan styles, featuring large domes, arches, and decorative tile work.

Intangible Heritage

The Bahmani court encouraged literature in Persian and Dakhni Urdu. Cultural exchange between northern Islamic traditions and southern Indian influences created a unique Deccan identity. Music, poetry, and calligraphy flourished under royal patronage.

Decline and Fragmentation

Internal conflicts between Deccani and foreign nobles weakened the central authority. After the execution of Mahmud Gawan, political instability increased. By the late 15th century, the Sultanate fragmented into five independent Deccan Sultanates: Ahmadnagar, Bijapur, Golconda, Bidar, and Berar. This marked the effective end of Bahmani unity.

Historical Sources and Evidence

Information about the Bahmani Sultanate comes from Persian chronicles, inscriptions, coins, and architectural remains. Structures in Gulbarga and Bidar provide archaeological evidence of their political and cultural achievements. Literary works of the period also document court life and administration.
