Quit India Movement (1942 CE)

Period: 1942 CE | Region: All India

Background

During World War II, the British involved India in the war without consulting Indian leaders or granting meaningful self-government. This created deep resentment among nationalists. The Cripps Mission (1942), sent to gain Indian support for the war, offered only limited and vague constitutional reforms after the war. The Indian National Congress rejected these proposals as unsatisfactory.

Economic hardship, rising prices, and food shortages during the war further increased public anger. Mahatma Gandhi concluded that only an immediate and uncompromising demand for British withdrawal could reflect the mood of the nation.

In July 1942, the Congress Working Committee met at Wardha and authorized Gandhi to launch a mass civil disobedience movement, which came to be known as the Quit India Movement.

Launch and Leadership

  • The Quit India Movement was launched by the Indian National Congress under the leadership of Mahatma Gandhi on 8 August 1942 at the All India Congress Committee session in Bombay (now Mumbai), held at Gowalia Tank Maidan (present-day August Kranti Maidan).
  • In his historic speech, Gandhi gave the call of “Do or Die”, urging Indians to either free the country or sacrifice their lives in the struggle for independence.
  • Major leaders such as Jawaharlal Nehru, Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel, and Maulana Abul Kalam Azad supported the movement and were arrested along with Gandhi in the early hours of 9 August 1942.

The British government responded with a massive crackdown. Almost the entire Congress leadership was arrested, and tens of thousands of activists were imprisoned. As a result, the movement was carried forward mainly by students, workers, young leaders, and local organizers.

Main Features

  • A nationwide mass civil disobedience movement demanding the immediate withdrawal of the British from India.
  • Creation of underground networks and student-led groups after the arrest of senior leaders to coordinate protests and resistance.
  • Strikes, boycotts, and sabotage of communication and transport systems, including damage to railway lines, telegraph wires, police stations, and government buildings, especially in Bihar, eastern Uttar Pradesh, Maharashtra, and Bengal.
  • Large-scale participation of students, workers, peasants, and sections of the urban middle class, making it one of the most widespread uprisings of the freedom struggle.

Although Gandhi emphasized strict non-violence, the spontaneous nature of protests and the absence of central leadership led to violent clashes in some regions. The British used these incidents to justify harsh repression.

Key Events

  • 8 August 1942: The All India Congress Committee passed the Quit India Resolution in Bombay, formally launching the movement.
  • 9 August 1942: Gandhi and other Congress leaders were arrested under the Defence of India Rules, leading to spontaneous demonstrations and strikes across major cities.
  • Widespread repression followed, with police firings, lathi-charges, and mass arrests, particularly in Bihar, eastern Uttar Pradesh, Bengal, and the Bombay Presidency.
  • Local parallel governments emerged in places such as Ballia (Uttar Pradesh), Satara (Maharashtra), and Tamluk (Bengal), where underground nationalist groups briefly took control of administration.

By 1944, the movement gradually declined due to severe repression and long imprisonments, but its political influence remained strong.

Impact and Significance

  • The Quit India Movement was the final mass movement of India’s freedom struggle and one of the most intense popular uprisings against British rule.
  • It showed the firm determination of Indians to achieve complete independence and shifted the demand from negotiation to immediate British withdrawal.
  • Although brutally suppressed, the scale of the movement convinced the British that their rule in India could not continue after the war.
  • The movement paved the way for later political developments that led to independence in 1947, including post-war negotiations and the transfer of power.
