Quit India Movement (1942 CE)

Period: 1942 CE | Region: All India

Background

During World War II, the British dragged India into the conflict without consulting Indian leaders or offering meaningful self‑government, creating deep resentment among the nationalist ranks. [web:204][web:208] The **Cripps Mission (1942)**, sent by the British government to secure Indian support for the war, proposed limited constitutional reforms after the war, but these were rejected by the Indian National Congress as inadequate and vague. [web:204][web:212] Economic hardship, inflation, and food shortages during the war further increased popular discontent, and Gandhi concluded that only an immediate and uncompromising demand for British withdrawal could match the mood of the nation. [web:204][web:209]

The Congress Working Committee met at **Wardha in July 1942** and authorised Gandhi to launch a mass civil disobedience movement, which later became the Quit India Movement. [web:204][web:207]

Launch and Leadership

  • Launched by the Indian National Congress under the leadership of Mahatma Gandhi on August 8, 1942 at the **All India Congress Committee session in Bombay (Mumbai)**, held at Gowalia Tank Maidan (now August Kranti Maidan). [web:204][web:207]
  • In his famous speech, Gandhi gave the call of “Do or Die”, urging Indians to either free the country or lay down their lives in the attempt, marking the most radical demand of the nationalist movement. [web:204][web:206]
  • Prominent leaders like Jawaharlal Nehru, Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel, Maulana Abul Kalam Azad, and others supported the movement and were arrested along with Gandhi in the early hours of **9 August 1942**. [web:204][web:207]

The British responded with a massive crackdown, arresting almost the entire Congress leadership and detaining tens of thousands of activists, which left the movement to be carried forward by younger leaders, students, workers, and local organisers. [web:204][web:208]

Main Features

  • Mass civil disobedience across the country demanding the **immediate withdrawal of the British** from India, framed as a final, uncompromising call for complete independence. [web:204][web:209]
  • Formation of underground networks and student‑led organisations after the arrest of top leaders, which coordinated protests, strikes, and sabotage in the absence of open leadership. [web:204][web:212]
  • Strikes, boycotts, and sabotage of communication and transport lines, including attacks on railway tracks, telegraph wires, police stations, and government buildings, especially in Bihar, eastern Uttar Pradesh, and parts of Maharashtra and Bengal. [web:204][web:208]
  • Participation from students, workers, peasants, and even some sections of the urban middle class in both rural and urban areas, making it one of the most widespread mass uprisings of the freedom struggle. [web:204][web:212]

Although Gandhi had stressed non‑violence, the spontaneous nature of the protests and the absence of central leadership led to violent clashes in many regions, which the British used to justify harsh repression. [web:204][web:208]

Key Events

  • 8 August 1942: The All India Congress Committee passed the **Quit India Resolution** at the Bombay session, formally launching the movement and demanding an end to British rule. [web:204][web:207]
  • 9 August 1942: Within hours, Gandhi and other members of the Congress Working Committee were arrested under the Defence of India Rules, triggering spontaneous demonstrations and hartals across major cities. [web:204][web:205]
  • Violent clashes and suppression followed across India, with the British using police firings, lathi‑charges, and mass detentions to crush the protests, especially in Bihar, eastern UP, Bombay Presidency, and Bengal. [web:204][web:208]
  • Emergence of local parallel governments in places like **Ballia (Uttar Pradesh), Satara (Maharashtra), and Tamluk (Bengal)**, where underground Congress‑linked groups took control of administration for short periods. [web:204][web:212]

The movement gradually declined by 1944 as repression intensified and many activists were imprisoned, but its political impact remained strong. [web:204][web:212]

Impact and Significance

  • The Quit India Movement marked the **final mass movement** in India’s struggle for freedom, representing the most intense and widespread popular uprising against British rule since 1857. [web:204][web:209]
  • It demonstrated the determination of Indians to achieve **complete independence**, shifting the nationalist discourse from negotiation to an uncompromising demand for immediate British withdrawal. [web:204][web:212]
  • Though suppressed by force, the scale and intensity of the movement convinced the British that their rule in India could not continue indefinitely and that post‑war negotiations would have to move toward transfer of power. [web:204][web:209]
  • The movement set the stage for the political developments that led to independence in **1947**, including the Cabinet Mission Plan, the partition of India, and the eventual end of colonial rule. [web:204][web:212]
