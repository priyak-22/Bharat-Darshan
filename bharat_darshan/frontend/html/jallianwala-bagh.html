Jallianwala Bagh Massacre (1919 CE)

Year: 1919 CE | Region: Amritsar, Punjab

Background

The massacre took place against the backdrop of the repressive Rowlatt Act (1919), which allowed the British government to detain Indians without trial and to suppress political activity. [web:174][web:178] The Act sparked widespread protests across India, especially in Punjab, where leaders such as Satyapal and Saifuddin Kitchlew were arrested and removed from Amritsar, further inflaming public anger. [web:175][web:182] On 10 April 1919, violent clashes broke out in Amritsar after a protest march was fired upon by British troops, leading to arson attacks and retaliatory repression. [web:175][web:178]

In this tense atmosphere, a large but peaceful crowd gathered at Jallianwala Bagh on 13 April 1919, the day of the Baisakhi festival, to protest the Rowlatt Act and to express their grievances against British rule. [web:174][web:176] The British administration, led by Lieutenant‑Governor Michael O’Dwyer, had already imposed martial law‑like restrictions in Amritsar, but many people were unaware that public meetings had been banned. [web:175][web:182]

The Incident

On April 13, 1919, thousands of men, women, and children gathered peacefully at Jallianwala Bagh, a walled garden in Amritsar, to attend a public meeting and celebrate Baisakhi. [web:174][web:176] British troops under General Reginald Dyer arrived, blocked the main narrow exit with his soldiers, and without issuing any warning or ordering the crowd to disperse, opened fire on the unarmed gathering. [web:175][web:179]

The firing continued for about **10–15 minutes**, during which around **1,650 rounds** were fired into the densely packed crowd. [web:175][web:179] People rushed to the exits, but many were shot as they tried to escape, and others jumped into a well to avoid bullets. [web:174][web:175] According to official British figures, about **379 people were killed** and **over 1,200 wounded**, though Indian estimates and later investigations suggest the death toll may have been **over 500 or even higher**. [web:174][web:175][web:178]

Aftermath

  • Widespread outrage and grief swept across India, as news of the massacre shocked both urban and rural populations and exposed the brutal face of colonial rule. [web:174][web:176]
  • Rabindranath Tagore renounced his knighthood in protest, writing a powerful letter to the Viceroy condemning the massacre and British policy. [web:174][web:176]
  • The British government set up the Hunter Commission to investigate the incident, which criticized Dyer’s actions but stopped short of imposing severe punishment. [web:175][web:178]
  • The event shattered Indian faith in British justice and marked a turning point in the freedom struggle, convincing many moderates that cooperation with the Raj was no longer possible. [web:176][web:182]

In Amritsar and surrounding areas, the British imposed harsh measures, including public floggings, collective fines, and humiliating punishments, which further deepened resentment against colonial rule. [web:175][web:182] The site of the massacre was later converted into a national memorial, with bullet‑marked walls and a martyrs’ well preserved as symbols of sacrifice. [web:174][web:181]

Impact and Significance

  • The massacre unified Indians across communities—Hindu, Muslim, Sikh, and others—in condemning British rule and demanding an end to colonial domination. [web:176][web:182]
  • It strengthened Mahatma Gandhi’s call for **Non‑Cooperation** and complete independence, as he shifted from cooperation with the British to mass civil disobedience after seeing the extent of state violence. [web:176][web:182]
  • The Jallianwala Bagh Massacre exposed the brutality of colonial rule to the world, drawing criticism even from some sections of British public opinion and contributing to long‑term pressure for Indian self‑government. [web:176][web:178]
  • The event remains a powerful symbol of resistance and sacrifice in India’s freedom struggle and is commemorated annually as a reminder of the cost of colonial repression. [web:174][web:181]
