Jallianwala Bagh Massacre (1919 CE)

Year: 1919 CE | Region: Amritsar, Punjab

Background

The massacre occurred in the context of the repressive Rowlatt Act (1919), which allowed the British government to arrest and detain Indians without trial and suppress political activities. This law triggered widespread protests across India, especially in Punjab.

In Amritsar, popular leaders Dr. Satyapal and Dr. Saifuddin Kitchlew were arrested and deported, which further angered the public. On 10 April 1919, clashes broke out after British troops fired on peaceful protestors, leading to violence and harsh retaliation by the authorities.

Amid this tense situation, a large but peaceful gathering assembled at Jallianwala Bagh on 13 April 1919, the day of the Baisakhi festival, to protest against the Rowlatt Act and express grievances against British rule. Many people were unaware that public meetings had been banned.

The Incident

On 13 April 1919, thousands of men, women, and children gathered at Jallianwala Bagh, a walled garden in Amritsar, to attend a public meeting and celebrate Baisakhi. British troops led by General Reginald Dyer entered the garden and blocked the main exit.

Without issuing any warning or ordering the crowd to disperse, Dyer commanded his soldiers to open fire on the unarmed gathering. The firing continued for about 10–15 minutes, during which nearly 1,650 rounds were fired into the densely packed crowd.

People ran toward the narrow exits, but many were shot while trying to escape. Some jumped into a well inside the garden to save themselves from bullets. According to official British records, about 379 people were killed and more than 1,200 were wounded. Indian estimates suggest that the number of deaths was much higher, possibly exceeding 500.

Aftermath

  • The massacre caused shock and anger throughout India and exposed the brutality of British colonial rule.
  • Rabindranath Tagore renounced his knighthood in protest and wrote a powerful letter condemning the British government.
  • The British government appointed the Hunter Commission to investigate the incident. Although it criticized Dyer’s actions, he was not severely punished.
  • The event destroyed Indian faith in British justice and became a major turning point in the freedom struggle.

In Amritsar and nearby regions, the British imposed harsh punishments such as public flogging, collective fines, and humiliating orders. These actions deepened resentment against colonial rule. Later, Jallianwala Bagh was converted into a national memorial, preserving the bullet-marked walls and the martyrs’ well.

Impact and Significance

  • The massacre united Indians of all communities—Hindus, Muslims, Sikhs, and others—against British rule.
  • It strengthened Mahatma Gandhi’s call for Non-Cooperation and mass civil disobedience.
  • The incident drew international attention and criticism of British colonial policies.
  • Jallianwala Bagh remains a powerful symbol of sacrifice and resistance and is commemorated every year in India’s freedom struggle.
