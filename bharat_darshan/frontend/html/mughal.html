Mughal Empire (1526–1857 CE)

Region: Northern, Central, and parts of Southern India — Capital cities included Agra, Delhi, and Fatehpur Sikri

Capital

The Mughal Empire had multiple capitals over time. Agra served as an early capital. Later, Emperor Akbar built Fatehpur Sikri . Under Shah Jahan, Delhi became the principal imperial capital.

Background

The Mughal Empire was founded by Babur in 1526 after his victory at the First Battle of Panipat against Ibrahim Lodi. Babur was a descendant of Timur and Genghis Khan. The empire gradually expanded under his successors, becoming one of the largest and most powerful empires in Indian history.

Founding and Expansion

After Babur, Humayun briefly lost the empire but regained it. The real consolidation happened under Akbar, who expanded Mughal rule across northern and central India. Later rulers such as Jahangir, Shah Jahan, and Aurangzeb extended Mughal authority to its greatest territorial limits.

Religion

The Mughal rulers were Muslims, but many adopted policies of tolerance. Akbar promoted religious dialogue and introduced the policy of Sulh-i-Kul (peace for all). Later rulers varied in their religious policies. The empire supported diverse traditions including Hinduism, Islam, Jainism, and Sikhism.

Economy and Administration

The Mughal economy was largely agrarian but supported flourishing trade and handicrafts. The Mansabdari system organized military and civil administration. Revenue reforms under Todar Mal improved tax assessment. India became a major exporter of textiles, spices, and precious goods.

Notable Rulers

  • Babur – Founder of the Mughal Empire.
  • Akbar – Expanded the empire and introduced administrative reforms.
  • Jahangir – Known for justice and patronage of art.
  • Shah Jahan – Builder of magnificent monuments.
  • Aurangzeb – Expanded the empire to its greatest size.

Political History and Achievements

The Mughal Empire achieved political stability and centralized governance. It maintained a vast bureaucracy and powerful army. Under Shah Jahan, Mughal architecture reached its peak, symbolized by the Taj Mahal . Mughal art, miniature painting, and literature flourished during this era.

Cultural Heritage

Tangible Heritage

The Mughal period produced remarkable monuments including the Taj Mahal, Humayun’s Tomb, Red Fort, Jama Masjid, and many gardens. Their architecture blended Persian, Central Asian, and Indian styles.

Intangible Heritage

The Mughals contributed to literature, music, cuisine, and language development. The Persian language flourished, and cultural synthesis led to the development of Indo-Islamic traditions.

Decline and Fall

After Aurangzeb’s death in 1707, the empire weakened due to internal conflicts, regional revolts, and external invasions. The rise of regional powers like the Marathas further reduced Mughal authority. In 1857, after the Revolt of 1857, the British formally ended the Mughal Empire.

Historical Sources and Evidence

Mughal history is documented through Persian chronicles, court records such as the Akbarnama, inscriptions, coins, architectural remains, and accounts of foreign travelers.
