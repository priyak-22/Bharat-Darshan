Non-Cooperation Movement (1920–1922 CE)

Period: 1920–1922 CE | Region: All India

Background

The Jallianwala Bagh Massacre (1919) and the repressive Rowlatt Act deeply angered Indians and shattered faith in British justice. [web:184][web:188] After World War I, Indians had expected political concessions for their wartime support, but instead faced continued repression, economic hardship, and the dismemberment of the Ottoman Empire, which hurt Muslim sentiment over the Khilafat issue. [web:184][web:189] Mahatma Gandhi, who had earlier cooperated with the British, lost faith in constitutional methods and decided to launch a nationwide campaign of peaceful non‑cooperation with British institutions. [web:185][web:190]

Gandhi linked the Non‑Cooperation Movement with the **Khilafat Movement**, uniting Hindus and Muslims in a common struggle against colonial rule. [web:184][web:186] His call for non‑violent resistance (satyagraha) aimed to withdraw Indian cooperation from every structure that sustained British power—administration, courts, schools, and the economy. [web:185][web:188]

Launch and Leadership

  • Launched by Mahatma Gandhi on 1 September 1920, after he issued a manifesto outlining the principles of non‑violent non‑cooperation. [web:185][web:186]
  • Supported by prominent leaders such as Motilal Nehru, Chittaranjan Das, Lala Lajpat Rai, Maulana Azad, and others who joined Gandhi in touring the country to mobilize support. [web:184][web:190]
  • The programme was formally adopted at the **special Calcutta Session of the Indian National Congress in September 1920** and later endorsed at the **Nagpur Session in December 1920**, making it the official Congress strategy. [web:184][web:186]

Gandhi promised that if the programme was fully implemented, India could achieve **Swaraj (self‑rule)** within a year, which energized the masses and gave the movement a clear political goal. [web:185][web:189]

Major Programmes

  • Boycott of British schools, colleges, courts, and government offices, with students leaving government institutions and lawyers giving up practice in British courts. [web:184][web:190]
  • Boycott of foreign goods and promotion of Swadeshi (use of Indian‑made goods), especially through the boycott of British textiles and liquor. [web:185][web:189]
  • Resignation from government posts, surrender of British titles and honors, and refusal to participate in official ceremonies and functions. [web:184][web:188]
  • Encouragement of Khadi and village industries, with Gandhi urging every household to spin and wear hand‑spun cloth as a symbol of self‑reliance and resistance. [web:185][web:186]

The movement also included public meetings, hartals (strikes), and picketing of liquor shops and foreign‑goods shops, which brought ordinary people into direct political action for the first time. [web:184][web:189]

Public Response

The movement spread rapidly across India, involving students, peasants, workers, lawyers, and women, and gave the freedom struggle a true mass character. [web:184][web:188] In many regions, people withdrew from government schools and courts, burned foreign cloth in public bonfires, and joined khadi‑spinning campaigns. [web:185][web:189] The participation of Muslims in the Khilafat–Non‑Cooperation alliance created unprecedented Hindu–Muslim unity and broadened the social base of the nationalist movement. [web:184][web:190]

However, the response was uneven: some landlords, officials, and sections of the elite remained cautious or opposed, while in a few areas the movement turned violent despite Gandhi’s emphasis on strict non‑violence. [web:184][web:193]

Suspension of the Movement

In February 1922, the Chauri Chaura incident in the Gorakhpur district of Uttar Pradesh, where a mob set fire to a police station and killed several policemen, led Gandhi to suspend the movement on 12 February 1922. [web:184][web:190] He believed that the outbreak of violence betrayed the core principle of non‑violence and that continuing the movement would risk turning it into a violent revolt. [web:185][web:188]

The British responded by arresting Gandhi and many Congress leaders, and by repressing the remaining activities of the movement. [web:184][web:190] Although the movement was formally called off, its impact on Indian political consciousness was already profound. [web:188][web:193]

Impact and Significance

  • The Non‑Cooperation Movement brought Gandhi to the forefront of Indian politics and established him as the undisputed leader of the national movement. [web:184][web:188]
  • It unified Indians across class, caste, and community lines, especially through the Hindu–Muslim alliance in the Khilafat–Non‑Cooperation phase. [web:184][web:190]
  • The movement marked the beginning of the Gandhian era in India’s freedom struggle, shifting the focus from elite‑based politics to mass‑based, non‑violent resistance. [web:185][web:188]
  • It prepared the ground for future mass movements such as the Civil Disobedience Movement and Quit India by creating a nationwide network of activists, volunteers, and organizations committed to Swaraj. [web:184][web:193]
