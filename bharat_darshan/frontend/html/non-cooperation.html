Non-Cooperation Movement (1920–1922 CE)

Period: 1920–1922 CE | Region: All India

Background

The Jallianwala Bagh Massacre (1919) and the repressive Rowlatt Act deeply angered Indians and destroyed their faith in British justice. After World War I, Indians had expected political concessions for their support, but instead faced continued repression, economic hardship, and disappointment over the dismemberment of the Ottoman Empire, which hurt Muslim sentiments over the Khilafat issue.

Mahatma Gandhi, who had earlier believed in cooperation with the British, lost faith in constitutional methods and decided to launch a nationwide campaign of peaceful non-cooperation with British institutions.

Gandhi linked the Non-Cooperation Movement with the Khilafat Movement, bringing Hindus and Muslims together in a common struggle against colonial rule. His idea of non-violent resistance (satyagraha) aimed to withdraw Indian support from every system that sustained British power, including administration, courts, schools, and the economy.

Launch and Leadership

  • The movement was launched by Mahatma Gandhi on 1 September 1920 through a manifesto explaining the principles of non-violent non-cooperation.
  • It was supported by major national leaders such as Motilal Nehru, Chittaranjan Das, Lala Lajpat Rai, Maulana Abul Kalam Azad, and others who toured the country to mobilize public support.
  • The programme was formally adopted at the Special Session of the Indian National Congress in Calcutta (September 1920) and later approved at the Nagpur Session (December 1920), making it the official Congress policy.

Gandhi promised that if the programme was fully followed, India could achieve Swaraj (self-rule) within one year. This gave the movement a clear political goal and inspired people across the country.

Major Programmes

  • Boycott of British schools, colleges, courts, and government offices. Students left government institutions and lawyers gave up their legal practice.
  • Boycott of foreign goods and promotion of Swadeshi, especially British cloth and liquor.
  • Resignation from government jobs and surrender of British titles and honors.
  • Promotion of Khadi and village industries. Gandhi encouraged every household to spin and wear hand-woven cloth as a symbol of self-reliance.

The movement also included public meetings, hartals (strikes), and picketing of liquor shops and foreign-goods stores, bringing ordinary people into active political participation for the first time.

Public Response

The movement spread rapidly throughout India and involved students, peasants, workers, lawyers, and women. It transformed the freedom struggle into a true mass movement. In many areas, people withdrew from government schools and courts, burned foreign cloth in public, and actively joined khadi-spinning campaigns.

The participation of Muslims through the Khilafat–Non-Cooperation alliance created a rare unity between Hindus and Muslims and widened the social base of the nationalist movement.

However, the response was not uniform. Some landlords, officials, and sections of the elite remained cautious or opposed the movement. In a few regions, violence occurred despite Gandhi’s strict emphasis on non-violence.

Suspension of the Movement

In February 1922, the Chauri Chaura incident in present-day Uttar Pradesh changed the course of the movement. A violent mob set fire to a police station and killed several policemen. Shocked by this event, Gandhi suspended the movement on 12 February 1922.

He believed that violence violated the basic principle of non-violence and that continuing the movement would turn it into an armed rebellion. The British government arrested Gandhi and many Congress leaders and suppressed remaining activities of the movement.

Although the movement ended, its influence on Indian political awareness and organization was long-lasting.

Impact and Significance

  • The Non-Cooperation Movement brought Mahatma Gandhi to the center of Indian politics and established him as the national leader.
  • It united Indians across class, caste, and religious lines, especially through the Hindu–Muslim alliance during the Khilafat phase.
  • The movement marked the beginning of the Gandhian era in the freedom struggle, shifting politics from elite leadership to mass participation.
  • It laid the foundation for later movements such as the Civil Disobedience Movement and Quit India Movement by creating a nationwide network of activists committed to Swaraj.
